Six ideas that explain everything the map and flame lab show. Start here if you have never studied combustion.

1.1

What a flame needs

3 min

THE IDEA

A flame needs three things at once: fuel, oxygen and enough heat to keep the reaction going. Take any one away and it goes out. Fire safety is built on this: prevent (keep heat sources and fuel apart, pick materials that resist burning), detect (notice smoke early) and suppress (remove oxygen or heat).

Many familiar flames are diffusion flames: fuel and oxygen start apart and only burn where they meet, in a thin glowing zone. A candle and a burning fuel droplet are examples. Smouldering is a separate form of combustion without a flame.

THE FORMULA

C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O + heat

Propane, one of NASA's test fuels. Every fuel uses up a fixed amount of oxygen as it burns. If the oxygen near the flame runs low, burning slows.

IN NASA'S DATA

Counting the tests...
Why it matters. NASA tests three kinds of fuel because they burn differently: gases (a leak), liquid droplets (a spray or a spill) and solids (fabric, plastic, wiring). A cabin is full of solids, so they matter most for safety.

Quick check: a fire extinguisher that floods the area with carbon dioxide works by removing which side of the triangle?

Oxygen. CO2 pushes the oxygen out and dilutes what is left. NASA's FLEX experiment tested exactly this with carbon dioxide and helium (lesson 1.6).

1.2

Gravity and the shape of fire

4 min

THE IDEA

On Earth, gas heated by a flame is lighter than the air around it, so it rises. Fresh air rushes in underneath to replace it. That flow stretches the flame into a teardrop, feeds it oxygen quickly and makes it flicker.

In microgravity (free fall), buoyancy is negligible. In still gas, oxygen reaches a flame by diffusion, and a flame around a round source can become spherical. Ventilation adds flow even when gravity is negligible.

THE FORMULA

Fb = (ρair − ρhot) × g

Fb is the upward push on each cubic metre of hot gas (N/m³). ρ is density, g is gravity. Hot gas is lighter because at the same pressure density falls as temperature rises: ρhot = ρair × Tair / Thot.

Set g = 0 and the push is zero. The model scales buoyancy with gravity and also approximates how flow changes fuel release. Those reaction and transport coefficients are illustrative.

Textbook values used here: ρair = 1.18 kg/m³ at 300 K, Thot = 1800 K. Rounded, not from NASA's tables.

0.0upward push, N/m³

Illustration: the shape follows the formula's direction, not a measured flame.

IN NASA'S DATA

Counting the tests...
Why it matters. A Moon Base sits at 1/6 of Earth's gravity, Mars at about 3/8. NASA's PSI database has no partial-gravity fire tests yet, so this collection cannot supply a measured result for either location. Experiments at the relevant gravity are needed.

Quick check: on the ISS, why can a flame go out without anyone touching it?

Right. With no buoyancy, burnt gas stays around the flame and fresh oxygen has to diffuse in. A flame that cannot get oxygen fast enough can smother itself. Fans on a spacecraft change this, which is why airflow matters so much in orbit.

1.3

Oxygen percent vs oxygen pressure

5 min

THE IDEA

Two numbers describe the oxygen in a cabin, and they mean different things:

  • Oxygen percent: what share of the gas molecules are oxygen. This drives how easily things burn.
  • Oxygen partial pressure: how hard the oxygen alone pushes. It is calculated from the oxygen fraction and total pressure. It is one quantity used in atmosphere comparisons.

A habitat at lower total pressure (good for spacesuit use, see the glossary) must raise the oxygen percent to keep the same partial pressure. Matching dry oxygen partial pressure can require a higher oxygen percentage. The fire evidence needs to account for both variables.

THE FORMULA

pO2 = (O2% / 100) × Ptotal

Sea-level air: 0.209 × 14.696 psi = 3.07 psi. Every dry atmosphere on this reference has pO2 = 3.07 psi. That calculation alone does not establish breathing suitability. On the map this is the dashed curve: O2% = 307 / Ptotal.

1.4

Why more oxygen burns hotter

3 min

THE IDEA

Nitrogen does not burn, but it is still there, soaking up heat. In Earth's air every oxygen molecule comes with almost four nitrogen molecules that the flame must warm up too. Fewer nitrogen molecules per oxygen means the flame's heat is shared among less gas, so the flame runs hotter, spreads faster, and is harder to put out.

THE FORMULA

N2 per O2 = (100 − O2%) / O2%

Earth air: 79 / 21 = 3.76. Moon Base: 70 / 30 = 2.33. Spacesuit: 0.

The flame lab turns this into a heat factor. From 21% oxygen up it follows the flame temperatures NASA calculated for its own propane tests, 2,271 K in 21.1% oxygen and 2,730 K in 39.5% (CFI-G, PSI-159): factor = 11.76 / (8 + N2 per O2). That gives 1.00 in Earth air, 1.14 at 30% and 1.47 in pure oxygen. NASA's rows stop at 40% oxygen, so the pure-oxygen figure is an extrapolation (site/sim.html, heatFactor).

Why it matters. Equal dry oxygen partial pressure does not establish equal fire behavior. Apollo 1's cabin was pure oxygen during a ground test, and a small fire spread through it in seconds.

Quick check: adding helium to a chamber (as NASA's FLEX tests did) helps put a flame out mainly because helium...

Right. Like nitrogen, helium does not burn. It lowers the oxygen share and takes heat from the flame.

1.5

Low pressure and mixing

3 min

THE IDEA

At lower pressure, gas molecules are further apart and collide less often, so each one travels further before it is knocked off course. Gases spread out faster: the diffusion coefficient rises as pressure falls. The gas is also thinner by the same factor, so the mass of oxygen that diffusion delivers to a flame each second stays about the same.

Lower pressure changes the flame in other ways. The chemistry runs slower, because molecules meet less often. Where there is gravity, buoyant flow has less effect. So a flame at 10 psi is a different flame from one at 14.7 psi, even at the same oxygen percent.

THE FORMULA

D ∝ 1 / P  ⇒  D / Dsea = 14.696 / P

D is how fast a gas spreads (the diffusion coefficient), at a fixed temperature. Halve the pressure and gases spread about twice as fast. Density ρ falls in step with pressure, so ρ × D, which sets how much oxygen diffusion carries, does not change.

Used in the flame lab: heat, motion and gases all spread as 1 / atm, with atm = psi / 14.696 (site/sim.html, flameParams). The lab's reaction speed does not change with pressure, which is one of its stated limits.

1.44×faster spreading than sea level
1.20×further in the same time (√)

Spread distance grows with the square root of D × time.

Why it matters. NASA's challenge statement expects a Moon Base at 10.2 psi or less, lower than the ISS. Oxygen percent and pressure would both differ from Earth's, and they act on a flame in different ways. A test is evidence for that cabin only when both are close.
1.6

How flames go out

4 min

THE IDEA

A flame survives only while it makes heat faster than it loses it. Lower the oxygen and the flame makes less heat; at some point losses win and it goes out by itself (self-extinguishes). An extinction boundary depends on the fuel and the test conditions, including airflow and pressure.

THE FORMULA

burns while  Qmade > Qlost

Qmade rises with oxygen (lessons 1.3 and 1.4). Qlost includes radiation, which in microgravity's slow, weak flames is a large share. This is a rule, not a calculation the site performs.

Read the chart with care. FLEX also changed the fuel, the pressure and the diluent gas between tests, so oxygen is not the only thing that differs between the bars. It is a pattern in real data, not a controlled experiment on oxygen alone.

2.2

The evidence rating

5 min

THE IDEA

For any point, Flashpoint asks: how many NASA tests ran near these conditions? It never asks an AI. It measures distance on the map and counts.

THE FORMULA

d = √( (ΔO2 / 10)² + (ΔP / 2)² )

ΔO2 is the oxygen difference in percentage points, ΔP the pressure difference in psi. Dividing by 10 and 2 makes 10 points of oxygen count the same as 2 psi. A test is near when d ≤ 1, which draws the dashed oval around your probe.

Used in: lib/evidence.ts (O2_SCALE = 10, P_SCALE = 2, NEAR_DISTANCE = 1, LIT_MIN_TESTS = 10).

WHY RATE EACH FUEL ON ITS OWN

A point can have many nearby droplet tests and completely untested with gas flames or cabin materials. One rating would hide that, so the site rates gas flames, droplets and solids separately. Click the Moon Base on the map in 2.1 to see exactly this: many droplet tests, no gas flames, no solids.

Quick check: a test at 30% oxygen and 12.2 psi, probe at 30% and 10.2 psi. Is it near?

ΔO2 = 0, ΔP = 2 psi, so d = √(0 + (2/2)²) = 1. Near counts d ≤ 1, so yes, just.

2.4

Advanced mode and the raw data

3 min

WHAT IT IS FOR

Advanced mode is for checking and reusing the evidence: every investigation, every test row, filters, graphs and a CSV download of exactly what you filtered. Every row keeps the NASA file and line it came from, so you can always go back to the source.

HOW THE NUMBERS WERE MADE

A script downloaded NASA's per-test tables and copied the values. Where NASA used other units, it converted them to psi:

psi = atm × 14.696  |  bar × 14.504  |  mmHg × 0.019337

Oxygen given as a fraction is multiplied by 100. Anything not stated is left empty, never estimated. Where NASA's table also gives the other gases in the chamber, the burn time, or its own words for how the test ended, the row keeps them.

Used in: scripts/build_tests.py; listed in data/tests.meta.json.

GOOD QUESTIONS TO ASK IT

  • Which fuels were tested above 30% oxygen? (filter oxygen, group by fuel)
  • Does a lower pressure change how often a flame went out? (graph pressure against outcome)
  • What are the exact conditions of the tests near the Moon Base? (map, then "See the tests around it")
For a report or a model: download the CSV and cite the PSI accession and source line for each row you use.

2.5

The flame lab: what comes from NASA

3 min

REAL, FROM NASA'S TEST

  • The oxygen, pressure and fuel of the test you pick, with the other gases in the chamber and the burn time where NASA's table gives them.
  • Whether NASA's flame lit and whether it went out. At the original atmosphere, 0 g and no added ventilation, the illustration follows that outcome when it is recorded.

REAL PHYSICS, IN THE EQUATIONS

  • Fuel burns only where it meets oxygen (lesson 1.1).
  • Buoyancy scales with gravity, Fb ∝ g (lesson 1.2). The model also approximates how flow changes fuel release.
  • Less oxygen, more nitrogen to heat, cooler flame (lesson 1.4). Between 20% and 40% oxygen the size of that effect follows the flame temperatures in NASA's tables.
  • Lower pressure, faster spreading of heat, motion and gases (lesson 1.5).

ILLUSTRATION

  • Apart from the heat curve, the model's constants were tuned by hand. One reaction stands in for every fuel, and its speed does not change with pressure.
  • Carbon dioxide and helium, which FLEX added to put flames out, are treated as nitrogen.
  • The colours of the 0 g flame are painted after NASA photographs.
  • Moon, Mars and Earth gravity: NASA ran every test at 0 g, so these show the same air with gravity added, not a measured result.

Glossary

Microgravity (0 g)
Free fall, as on the ISS. Objects float and hot gas does not rise.
Partial gravity
Between 0 g and 1 g: the Moon (0.166 g), Mars (0.378 g). No partial-gravity tests in this collection.
Diffusion flame
A flame where fuel and oxygen start apart and burn where they meet.
Inverse flame
Oxygen flows out of the burner into a fuel-filled chamber, the reverse of usual. Its oxygen comes from the burner, so the map leaves it out by default.
Oxygen partial pressure
The oxygen fraction multiplied by total pressure. The site compares dry-gas values.
psi
Pounds per square inch. Sea-level air is 14.7 psi.
Pre-breathing
Breathing pure oxygen before a spacewalk to clear nitrogen from the body and avoid the bends. Lower cabin pressure shortens it.
Self-extinguish
A flame going out on its own because it loses heat faster than it makes it.
Kept burning
The site's label for a test where the flame did not go out by itself. It burned until the droplet was used up (FLEX: "Completion"), until NASA stopped the fuel flow (CFI-G: "FT") or for the burn time listed (SAFFIRE).
Diluent
A gas that does not burn, mixed into the atmosphere. Cabin air uses nitrogen. FLEX also added carbon dioxide or helium to study how they put flames out.
Flammability limit
The lowest oxygen at which a material keeps burning.
PSI accession
NASA Physical Science Informatics record number, for example PSI-69 for FLEX. Every test row cites one.
Investigation
One NASA experiment campaign, such as FLEX or BASS-II, made up of many tests.
