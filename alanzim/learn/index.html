1.1

What a flame needs

3 min

THE IDEA

A flame needs three things at once: fuel, oxygen and enough heat to keep the reaction going. Take any one away and it goes out. Fire safety is built on this: prevent (keep heat sources and fuel apart, pick materials that resist burning), detect (notice smoke early) and suppress (remove oxygen or heat).

Most fires in a cabin are diffusion flames: fuel and oxygen start apart and only burn where they meet, in a thin glowing zone. A candle, a burning droplet and a smouldering fabric all work this way.

THE FORMULA

C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O + heat

Propane, one of NASA's test fuels. Every fuel uses up a fixed amount of oxygen as it burns. If the oxygen near the flame runs low, burning slows.

IN NASA'S DATA

Counting the tests...
Why it matters. NASA tests three kinds of fuel because they burn differently: gases (a leak), liquid droplets (a spray or a spill) and solids (fabric, plastic, wiring). A cabin is full of solids, so they matter most for safety.

Quick check: a fire extinguisher that floods the area with carbon dioxide works by removing which side of the triangle?

Oxygen. CO2 pushes the oxygen out and dilutes what is left. NASA's FLEX experiment tested exactly this with carbon dioxide and helium (lesson 1.6).

1.2

Gravity and the shape of fire

4 min

THE IDEA

On Earth, gas heated by a flame is lighter than the air around it, so it rises. Fresh air rushes in underneath to replace it. That flow stretches the flame into a teardrop, feeds it oxygen quickly and makes it flicker.

In microgravity (free fall), nothing is "lighter" any more, so hot gas does not rise. Oxygen only reaches the flame by slowly diffusing in. The flame becomes a small, steady, dim blue sphere.

THE FORMULA

Fb = (ρair − ρhot) × g

Fb is the upward push on each cubic metre of hot gas (N/m³). ρ is density, g is gravity. Hot gas is lighter because at the same pressure density falls as temperature rises: ρhot = ρair × Tair / Thot.

Set g = 0 and the push is zero. That one term is the only thing gravity changes in the Sim.

Textbook values used here: ρair = 1.18 kg/m³ at 300 K, Thot = 1800 K. Rounded, not from NASA's tables.

0.0upward push, N/m³

Illustration: the shape follows the formula's direction, not a measured flame.

IN NASA'S DATA

Counting the tests...
Why it matters. A Moon Base sits at 1/6 of Earth's gravity, Mars at about 3/8. NASA's PSI database has no partial-gravity fire tests yet, so for those places the honest answer is "somewhere between the 0 g and 1 g results". Flashpoint says that out loud instead of guessing.

Quick check: on the ISS, why can a flame go out without anyone touching it?

Right. With no buoyancy, burnt gas stays around the flame and fresh oxygen has to diffuse in. A flame that cannot get oxygen fast enough can smother itself. Fans on a spacecraft change this, which is why airflow matters so much in orbit.

1.3

Oxygen percent vs oxygen pressure

5 min

THE IDEA

Two numbers describe the oxygen in a cabin, and they mean different things:

  • Oxygen percent: what share of the gas molecules are oxygen. This drives how easily things burn.
  • Oxygen partial pressure: how hard the oxygen alone pushes. This is what your lungs care about: it decides whether you can breathe.

A habitat at lower total pressure (good for spacesuit use, see the glossary) must raise the oxygen percent to keep the same partial pressure. Same breathing, more flammable air. That trade is the whole reason Flashpoint exists.

THE FORMULA

pO2 = (O2% / 100) × Ptotal

Sea-level air: 0.209 × 14.696 psi = 3.07 psi. Every atmosphere with pO2 = 3.07 psi breathes like sea level. On the map this is the dashed curve: O2% = 307 / Ptotal.

Used in: site/assets/app.js (SEA_PPO2 = 3.07), site/sim.html (Oxygen pressure row).

Quick check: an atmosphere of 40% oxygen at 7.7 psi. Compared with sea level, is it easier to breathe, the same, or harder?

About the same: 0.40 × 7.7 = 3.08 psi, right on the sea-level curve. But at 40% oxygen it is far more flammable.

1.4

Why more oxygen burns hotter

3 min

THE IDEA

Nitrogen does not burn, but it is still there, soaking up heat. In Earth's air every oxygen molecule comes with almost four nitrogen molecules that the flame must warm up too. Fewer nitrogen molecules per oxygen means the flame's heat is shared among less gas, so the flame runs hotter, spreads faster, and is harder to put out.

THE FORMULA

N2 per O2 = (100 − O2%) / O2%

Earth air: 79 / 21 = 3.76. Moon Base: 70 / 30 = 2.33. Spacesuit: 0.

The Sim turns this into a flame-heat factor: heat ∝ 16 / (1 + 3.15 / x), where x is the oxygen share. It equals 1 in Earth air and is capped at 1.5. The direction is real; the size is tuned by hand (site/sim.html, flameParams).

Why it matters. This is why "same breathing" (lesson 1.3) does not mean "same fire risk". Apollo 1's cabin was pure oxygen during a ground test, and a small fire spread through it in seconds.

Quick check: adding helium to a chamber (as NASA's FLEX tests did) helps put a flame out mainly because helium...

Right. Like nitrogen, helium does not burn. It lowers the oxygen share and takes heat from the flame.

1.5

Low pressure and mixing

3 min

THE IDEA

At lower pressure, gas molecules are further apart and bump into each other less, so they spread out faster. In microgravity, where spreading (diffusion) is the only way oxygen reaches a flame, this matters a lot: at low pressure the flame gets its oxygen from further away, grows larger and dimmer.

THE FORMULA

D ∝ 1 / P  ⇒  D / Dsea = 14.696 / P

D is how fast a gas spreads (the diffusion coefficient), at a fixed temperature. Halve the pressure and gases spread about twice as fast.

Used in the Sim: mixing = 0.2 / atm, with atm = psi / 14.696 (site/sim.html, flameParams).

1.44×faster spreading than sea level
1.20×further in the same time (√)

Spread distance grows with the square root of D × time.

IN NASA'S DATA

Counting the tests...
Why it matters. A Moon Base runs at about 10.2 psi, lower than the ISS. Its fires would mix differently, which is why tests at the right pressure, not just the right oxygen percent, are the evidence that counts.
1.6

How flames go out

4 min

THE IDEA

A flame survives only while it makes heat faster than it loses it. Lower the oxygen and the flame makes less heat; at some point losses win and it goes out by itself (self-extinguishes). The oxygen level where this happens is a material's flammability limit, the single most useful number for choosing cabin materials.

THE FORMULA

burns while  Qmade > Qlost

Qmade rises with oxygen (lessons 1.3 and 1.4). Qlost includes radiation, which in microgravity's slow, weak flames is a large share. This is a rule, not a calculation the site performs.

Read the chart with care. FLEX also changed the fuel, the pressure and the diluent gas between tests, so oxygen is not the only thing that differs between the bars. It is a pattern in real data, not a controlled experiment on oxygen alone.

Quick check: in the chart, what happens to the share of droplets that went out by themselves as oxygen rises?

It falls. In low oxygen almost every flame went out on its own; at Moon Base oxygen levels very few did. That is the fire-risk side of the Moon Base trade, in NASA's own numbers.

2.2

The evidence rating

5 min

THE IDEA

For any point, Flashpoint asks: how many NASA tests ran near these conditions? It never asks an AI. It measures distance on the map and counts.

THE FORMULA

d = √( (ΔO2 / 10)² + (ΔP / 2)² )

ΔO2 is the oxygen difference in percentage points, ΔP the pressure difference in psi. Dividing by 10 and 2 makes 10 points of oxygen count the same as 2 psi. A test is near when d ≤ 1, which draws the dashed oval around your probe.

Used in: lib/evidence.ts (O2_SCALE = 10, P_SCALE = 2, NEAR_DISTANCE = 1, LIT_MIN_TESTS = 10).

WHY RATE EACH FUEL ON ITS OWN

A point can be well tested with droplets and completely untested with gas flames or cabin materials. One rating would hide that, so the site rates gas flames, droplets and solids separately. Click the Moon Base on the map in 2.1 to see exactly this: many droplet tests, no gas flames, no solids.

Quick check: a test at 30% oxygen and 12.2 psi, probe at 30% and 10.2 psi. Is it near?

ΔO2 = 0, ΔP = 2 psi, so d = √(0 + (2/2)²) = 1. Near counts d ≤ 1, so yes, just.

2.4

Advanced mode and the raw data

3 min

WHAT IT IS FOR

Advanced mode is for checking and reusing the evidence: every investigation, every test row, filters, graphs and a CSV download of exactly what you filtered. Every row keeps the NASA file and line it came from, so you can always go back to the source.

HOW THE NUMBERS WERE MADE

A script downloaded NASA's per-test tables and copied the values. Where NASA used other units, it converted them to psi:

psi = atm × 14.696  |  bar × 14.504  |  mmHg × 0.019337

Oxygen given as a fraction is multiplied by 100. Anything not stated is left empty, never estimated.

Used in: scripts/build_tests.py; listed in data/tests.meta.json.

GOOD QUESTIONS TO ASK IT

  • Which fuels were tested above 30% oxygen? (filter oxygen, group by fuel)
  • Does a lower pressure change how often a flame went out? (graph pressure against outcome)
  • What are the exact conditions of the tests near the Moon Base? (map, then "See the tests around it")
For a report or a model: download the CSV and cite the PSI accession and source line for each row you use.

2.5

The Sim: what is real and what is illustration

3 min

REAL, FROM NASA'S TEST

  • The oxygen, pressure and fuel of the test you pick.
  • Whether NASA's flame lit and whether it went out. At 0 g the Sim follows NASA's record.

REAL PHYSICS, IN THE EQUATIONS

  • Fuel burns only where it meets oxygen (lesson 1.1).
  • Gravity only adds buoyancy, Fb ∝ g (lesson 1.2).
  • Less oxygen, more nitrogen to heat, cooler flame (lesson 1.4).
  • Lower pressure, faster mixing (lesson 1.5).

ILLUSTRATION

  • The model's constants were tuned by hand, not fitted to NASA measurements.
  • The colours of the 0 g flame are painted after NASA photographs.
  • Moon, Mars and Earth gravity: NASA ran every test at 0 g, so these show the same air with gravity added, not a measured result.
How to use it: to build intuition about why a flame looks and behaves the way it does. Not to read off numbers for a design.

Glossary

Microgravity (0 g)
Free fall, as on the ISS. Objects float and hot gas does not rise.
Partial gravity
Between 0 g and 1 g: the Moon (0.166 g), Mars (0.378 g). No fire data in PSI yet.
Diffusion flame
A flame where fuel and oxygen start apart and burn where they meet.
Inverse flame
Oxygen flows out of the burner into a fuel-filled chamber, the reverse of usual. Its oxygen comes from the burner, so the map leaves it out by default.
Oxygen partial pressure
The pressure of the oxygen alone. Decides breathing.
psi
Pounds per square inch. Sea-level air is 14.7 psi.
Pre-breathing
Breathing pure oxygen before a spacewalk to clear nitrogen from the body and avoid the bends. Lower cabin pressure shortens it.
Self-extinguish
A flame going out on its own because it loses heat faster than it makes it.
Flammability limit
The lowest oxygen at which a material keeps burning.
PSI accession
NASA Physical Science Informatics record number, for example PSI-69 for FLEX. Every test row cites one.
Investigation
One NASA experiment campaign, such as FLEX or BASS-II, made up of many tests.
